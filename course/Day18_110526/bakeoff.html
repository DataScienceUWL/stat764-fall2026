Try first: answer each in your head, then open it

1. What does each new boosted tree fit?

What is still wrong: the outcome minus the predicted chance for a yes/no outcome, the residual for a number.

2. Why can more rounds hurt boosting, when more trees never hurt a forest?

A forest averages: another tree only steadies the average. Boosting adds: every round fits the training data more closely, and eventually it is fitting noise.

3. As the rounds pile up, what goes wrong first: the order (AUC) or the probabilities (calibration slope)?

The probabilities. In Tuesday’s studio, test AUC held through 300 rounds (0.647, 0.653, 0.652) while the calibration slope fell from 1.02 to 0.80, and to 0.59 by round 1,000.

How the trees add up

Chances cannot simply be added: they would pass 100%. So boosting adds on the log-odds scale, then converts back. This stay starts at log-odds −2.03 (11.6%). Tree 1 sends it to a leaf of +1.99, and 0.1 × 1.99 = +0.20 moves it to −1.83, which is 13.8%. After all 34 trees it is at −0.80, which is 31.1%, and that is exactly what predict_proba reports. The steps are worked on Meeting 17’s page.

Part 1 · the idea, smallIs a gap real?

Sixty made-up patients. Model A beats Model B. Would it on other patients?

Is Model A really better?

No. We built these two models to be equally good, so the whole gap of 0.038 is luck. It still looked like a win.

Why redraw patients, and why keep the pair?

Redraw because the test patients are one sample, and a different sample would give a different gap. Patients, not stays, because a patient’s stays belong together (Meeting 9). Keep the pair, both models on the same redraw, so the luck they share cancels: a difficult redraw is difficult for both. Comparing two separate intervals would miss that and look less certain than it is.

Part 2 · the real dataFour models, one scoreboard

The fourth model: what is TabICL?

A foundation model is trained once, on a huge amount of data, then used on problems it has never seen.

You have met the idea: ChatGPT and Claude are foundation models for text. TabICL is one for tables, and your training rows play the part of the prompt.

TabICL is a neural network with about 28 million weights. We treat it as a black box here; how such networks work is a topic for DS 776.

Where the learning happens
questionlogistic regression, forest, boostingTabICL
When does it learn?when you call fitonce, by its makers, before you use it
From what?your training rowsabout 35 million simulated tables
What does fit do?finds coefficients, grows treeskeeps your rows as context
What must you tune?a little to a lotnothing
How can it predict without training on your data?
  • Practice. Make up a table, hide some outcomes, predict them from the rest, adjust. It did that about 35 million times. It learned a way of reading tables, not any one table.
  • On your data. One pass through the network, with nothing updated. It sums up each row, then each test patient looks at the training patients and their outcomes. That is the ICL in the name: in-context learning.
  • Think k-nearest neighbors. fit keeps the rows and the work happens at prediction. The difference is that TabICL learned in advance how to use those rows.
  • Eight tries. By default it runs 8 times, with the columns shuffled, and averages the answers.
What does it know about patients?

Nothing. It trained only on simulated tables, so it has never seen a hospital. It does not read column names either: number_inpatient is just a column of numbers. Everything it knows about these patients comes from the 15,000 you hand it.

Who made it?

The Soda team at Inria, in France. We ran TabICLv2, published at ICML 2026, with open code and weights. Its best-known relative is TabPFN.

Reading the columns
  • AUC is the order. Brier is the numbers and the order together; lower is better.
  • Average said should match the 11.1% who came back. TabICL’s 9.8% runs low.
  • Calibration slope: 1 means the spread is right; below 1, too extreme.
  • Net per 1,000 is Meeting 10’s money: visit everyone at or above 0.169, the break-even for a $150 visit that prevents 5% of a $17,700 readmission.
