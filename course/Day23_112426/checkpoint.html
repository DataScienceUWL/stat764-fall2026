Before you upload · about five minutesConnecting the dots: honest numbers

Try first: answer each in your head, then open it

1. Stays with no A1C test came back more often (11.7% against about 9.9%). Drop them, fill them, or keep the blank?

Keep the blank as information: a “not measured” category, or a “was blank” column. Dropping them changes who the model is about; filling them hides that the blank itself predicts.

2. You pick the threshold with the lowest cost on your test months. What is wrong?

The threshold is a tuned choice, so the test months stop being a fair test. Choose it on out-of-fold predictions from your resampling; apply it to the test months once, at the end.

3. You tried 30 models and report the best cross-validated score. Is it honest?

A little optimistic: the best of 30 noisy averages won partly by luck. Say how many you tried and give the fold-to-fold spread; the honest number is the test months, scored once.

Why is the best of many optimistic?

Every fold-average has some luck in it. Try 30 models and the winner is the best one plus, usually, some of the best luck. On new data the luck does not come along, so the winner looks a little worse. That is why the test months are scored once, after every choice is made.

Part 2 · the ideaTwo made-up numbers, one ratio

A threshold rule depends on your prices only through their ratio. Sweep the ratio, not each price.

The readmission decision from Meetings 18–20: a follow-up visit costs $150 and prevents some share of the readmissions among the patients it reaches; a readmission costs $17,700. The share was made up (5%). Visit when

chance of coming back ≥ cost of a visit ÷ (share prevented × $17,700) = the break-even

Your capstone’s version

Two error costs (a false alarm and a miss): act when the chance is at least false-alarm cost ÷ (false-alarm cost + miss cost). With $50 and $200, that is 50 ÷ 250 = 0.20; $100 and $400 give the same 0.20. Sweep that ratio. A capacity (act on at most K a day): sweep K instead, and report how many of the K were real.

The verdicts run in diagonal bands because each depends only on the break-even. A finer sweep puts the edges around our guess at about $50 and $230 a visit (at 5% prevented), or about 3% and 15% prevented (at $150).

The sentence for your final reportWe recommend visiting the high-risk stays as long as a $150 visit prevents between about 3% and 15% of readmissions. Below that, too few stays qualify for the gain to be real; above it, visiting everyone does as well.
How each verdict is decided

For each break-even, the 24,706 test stays are redrawn 300 times by patient, as in Meeting 20. High-risk: the model’s net and its edge over visiting everyone both stay above zero in the middle 95%. Everyone: the edge’s band includes zero, or the model visits 99% of stays anyway. Not clearly: the model’s own net band includes zero; at a break-even of 0.265 it visits only 4% of stays.

See it in code

# p: each stay's chance of coming back; y: 1 if it came back
# net: per 1,000 stays; multiply by share × $17,700 for dollars
for t in np.arange(0.02, 0.40, 0.01):        # sweep the break-even
    visit = p >= t
    net = ((y - t) * visit).mean() * 1000
    print(f"{t:.2f}  visited {visit.mean():5.1%}  net {net:6.1f}")
