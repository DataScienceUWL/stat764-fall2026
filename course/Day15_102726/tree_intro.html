Growing a decision tree

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions that the computer writes from past cases. This page builds one on 20 patients you can see one by one, then on 74,637 real hospital stays, then for predicting a number instead of a yes or no. It ends with a guide to today’s studio.

AskFind the single yes/no question that best separates the patients who came back from those who did not.
SplitDivide the patients into the “yes” group and the “no” group.
RepeatDo the same thing inside each group, separately, until a stopping rule says stop.

Part 1Twenty patients you can see

Here are 20 made-up hospital patients. For each we know two things: how many times they were in the hospital in the past year (prior stays) and how long this stay lasted (days in the hospital). We also know the outcome: did they come back within 30 days? Seven of the 20 did.

The tree’s job is to sort these patients into groups that are as alike as possible: groups where nearly everyone came back, or nearly no one did.

Step 1How mixed is a group?

The tree needs a number for how mixed a group is. The usual one is the Gini impurity. If a share p of a group came back,

Gini = 2 · p · (1 − p)

It is 0 when the group is pure (no one came back, or everyone did) and largest, 0.5, when the group is half and half. Move the slider to see it.

Our 20 patients: 7 came back, so p = 0.35 and the Gini is 2 × 0.35 × 0.65 = 0.455. Every question the tree asks will be judged by how far it brings that number down.

Step 2List every yes/no question

A tree asks one kind of question: is this column more than some cut? For a column of numbers, the cuts sit halfway between neighboring values in the data. Prior stays run from 0 to 5, so there are 5 possible questions (more than 0? more than 1? … more than 4?). Days in the hospital take 13 different values, so there are 12 more. That makes 17 candidate questions.

Step 3Score every question

A question splits the patients into a “no” side and a “yes” side. Work out each side’s Gini. Then average the two, weighting each side by its share of the patients, so a big group counts more than a small one. The drop from 0.455 is the question’s reduction in impurity. Bigger is better.

Try any question below, or pick one from the list on the right.

Step 4Keep the winner

The best question is “more than 1 prior stay?” The 12 patients with 0 or 1 prior stays include just 1 who came back (Gini 0.153). The 8 with more include 6 who came back (Gini 0.375). Weighted, the impurity falls from 0.455 to 0.242, a reduction of 0.213. The runner-up, more than 3 prior stays?, manages 0.149. The winner becomes the first question of the tree, its root.

Step 5Repeat inside each group, separately

Now each group gets its own fresh search over all the questions, using only its own patients. The two groups can end up asking different questions, or the same column at a different cut. The first question never changes afterward: a tree never goes back to revise an earlier choice. Step through it.

After three questions there are four groups, called leaves. Among patients with 0 or 1 prior stays, the tree asks more than 10 days?; among those with more, it asks more than 6 days? The same column, a different cut on each side.

Step 6Know when to stop

Press Next split to the end and the tree keeps going until every group is pure, some of them a single patient. That tree is right about every one of these 20 patients and has learned nothing that carries over to new ones: it has memorized them. So we stop it early, with a rule such as at most this many questions in a row (the depth) or no group smaller than this. The right setting is chosen by cross-validation, like any other tuning setting.

Step 7Predict for a new patient

A new patient answers the questions from the top until there are none left. The prediction is the share of that leaf’s past patients who came back. Set up a new patient and watch the path.

A leaf’s share is a fact about past patients. Whether it works as a probability for future ones (do patients told 20% come back about 20% of the time?) is a separate check, called calibration. Shares from leaves of 3 or 4 patients are far too noisy to trust.

Boxes against a line

Where does a model switch from “won’t come back” to “will”? Shade every spot on the plot by the model’s predicted chance, and draw a thick line where that chance reaches 50%. Here are three models fit to the same 20 patients: the tree after three questions, the tree grown all the way, and a logistic regression on the same two columns.

What this shows. Both trees cut the plot into boxes, so their lines are made only of vertical and horizontal pieces. The tree after three questions draws its line at more than 1 prior stay and gets 3 of the 20 patients wrong. The tree grown all the way gets none wrong, by fencing off single patients: a thin strip around the patient with 1 prior stay and 11 days, who came back, and a notch around the two patients with 2 or 3 prior stays and 5 or 6 days, who did not. Each of those boxes rests on one or two patients. Logistic regression draws one straight line, slanted because it weighs both columns at once: in its fit, one more prior stay counts as much as about 6.4 more days in the hospital. It also gets 3 wrong.

Each training stay has 20 columns: 8 counts (prior stays, days in the hospital, medications, lab tests, procedures, diagnoses, outpatient and emergency visits) and 12 categories (age band, admission type, where the patient was sent on discharge, medical specialty, and others).

Steps 1–2 The whole group is mixed but not evenly: only 11.5% came back, so the Gini is 2 × 0.1149 × 0.8851 = 0.2034, well below the 0.5 of a half-and-half group. The 420 questions are 308 cuts on the 8 count columns and one question for each of the 112 categories (sent to a rehab facility? age 70–80?).

How a category becomes a question. scikit-learn’s trees can only ask is this number at most a cut? So each category column is first turned into one 0/1 column per category (one-hot encoding), and a question about a category asks whether a patient is in that one category or not. The 12 category columns become 112 such columns.

  • Codes are categories, not amounts. Discharge code 22 (sent to a rehab facility) is not “more” than code 3, so code columns are treated as text and never cut like counts.
  • Missing is its own category. A1Cresult_nan means no A1C result was recorded, which can itself be informative.
  • Rare categories are pooled. Categories with fewer than 20 training stays share one column ending in _infrequent_sklearn.
  • One category against the rest. Each question singles out one category. Separating several takes several questions in a row.

Steps 3–4 Each question is scored exactly as in Part 1. Below is the best question from each of the 20 columns, ranked by its reduction in impurity.

The winner splits the 74,637 stays into 64,018 with 0 or 1 prior stays (9.7% came back) and 10,619 with 2 or more (22.5% came back). Its score, from the two sides’ Ginis:

(64,018 × 0.1745 + 10,619 × 0.3491) ÷ 74,637 = 0.1993

Step 5 Then each side gets its own search. Among the 64,018, the best question is a category, sent to a rehab facility? At the top it ranked third. Among the 10,619 with 2 or more prior stays, the best question is prior stays again, at a higher cut.

Step 6 Grown deeper, the tree first gets better on new patients, then worse. With no limit it makes 11,560 groups, scores a perfect AUC of 1.000 on its own patients, and only 0.524 on new ones, barely better than a coin flip. Cross-validation on the training patients picks a depth of 6.

Step 7 A new patient answers the questions to a leaf. In the two-question tree above, a patient with 3 prior stays answers yes to “more than 1?” and no to “more than 4?”, landing with 9,103 past stays: the tree says 20.0%. Every patient in that leaf gets exactly 20.0%, whatever else is true of them.

That is the price of a tree. The depth-6 tree gives the 24,706 test patients only 44 different probabilities. Logistic regression, a formula, gives nearly every patient their own.

Part 3Trees that predict a number

Everything so far predicted a yes/no outcome. A regression tree predicts a number instead: how many days a stay will last, or what a house will sell for. It follows the same seven steps. Only two things change: how it measures a mixed group, and what a leaf predicts.

a yes/no outcomea number
How mixed is a group?Gini impurity, 2p(1 − p) Its spread: the average squared distance from the group’s average, the group’s MSE
A question’s scoreThe two sides’ Gini, weighted by sizeThe two sides’ MSE, weighted by size
A leaf predictsThe share of its cases who came backThe average of its cases

The MSE is the one you already know: predict every patient in a group with the group’s average, then average the squared errors. A group where everyone stayed the same number of days has an MSE of 0, the counterpart of a pure group.

Here are 16 new made-up patients with one column, the number of procedures during the stay, and a number to predict, days in the hospital. Together they average 6.3 days, with an MSE of 12.46. Try a question.

The best question is “more than 3 procedures?” It leaves 11 patients averaging 4.2 days (MSE 2.69) and 5 averaging 11.0 days (MSE 2.00). Weighted, the MSE falls from 12.46 to 2.48. Then, as before, each side gets its own search. On the left, more than 1 procedure? separates stays of about 3 days from stays of about 6. On the right, no question helps: every cut leaves both sides averaging 11 days, a reduction of 0, so that group stays a leaf.

In code the only change is the class: DecisionTreeRegressor in place of DecisionTreeClassifier. It scores questions by squared error unless told otherwise, and takes the same stopping rules.

One tree is unstable

Back to the readmission data. Grow the same two-question tree on two random samples of 1,000 training stays. Same kind of patients, same method, and the first question is not even the same:

Sample 1 · first questionMore than 3 prior stays?
Sample 2 · first questionAge 80–90?

Because every later question depends on the first, a small change in the data can change the whole tree, and the story a reader would tell from it.

Question 1: how to call split_gini

For one question, count each side’s patients and how many of them came back, then call

split_gini(came back on one side, that side’s size, came back on the other side, its size)

The order of the two sides does not matter. The answer is the size-weighted Gini of Part 1, Step 3: lower is better. The notebook works one example for you, on a question that is not one of the four.

Folds split by patient

Some patients were in the hospital several times. If one of a patient’s stays is in the fold being scored while another is in the data the model was fit on, the model has partly seen that patient, and the cross-validated AUC looks better than it will on new patients. GroupKFold(5) keeps all of a patient’s stays in the same fold, and groups=patients[train] inside .fit(...) tells it which stays belong to whom. Section 4 of the notebook does this for the depth; question 2 does the same for min_samples_leaf.

min_samples_leaf, the other stopping rule

It is the smallest group the tree is allowed to make. A question that would leave a side smaller than this is never asked. The larger the setting, the fewer and bigger the leaves: each leaf’s share rests on more patients, and the tree gives fewer different probabilities. Like the depth, it is chosen by cross-validation.

The calibration table, by_decile

It sorts the test patients by their predicted chance, splits them into about ten groups, and shows two numbers for each group: said, the average prediction, and came back, the share who actually did. When the two are close, the probabilities can be trusted. A tree gives only a few different numbers, so some groups merge and you may see fewer than ten rows.

A sample drawn with replacement

rng.integers(0, len(train), 1_000) picks 1,000 training rows at random, and the same row can be picked twice. Each draw is a new sample of the kind you might have collected instead. Make rng once, before the loop; made inside the loop, it draws the same sample every time.
