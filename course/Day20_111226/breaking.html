How the figure is made

For each share a visit might prevent, from 1% to 25%:

  1. Break-even: $150 ÷ (share × $17,700). At 5% that is 0.169.
  2. Apply the model: visit every test stay whose predicted chance is at least the break-even.
  3. Net per 1,000 stays: readmissions among the visited × share × $17,700, minus $150 per visit, scaled to 1,000 stays. “Visit everyone” is the same sum with every stay visited.
  4. Bands: redraw the test patients 300 times (each with all their stays, as in Meeting 18), redo steps 2–3, and keep the middle 95%.
  5. “No real gap”: the first share where the middle 95% of model minus everyone includes 0.

Part 1 · look-alike columns · breaks permutation importanceIs something else carrying the same information?

Add a near-copy of prior stays, and each looks a third as important. Drop either one and the forest loses nothing; drop both and it loses 0.036.

“Prior stays: 0.018” is right about the model, and invites the wrong reading, that prior stays barely matters.

In practice: before you read an importance table, look for columns that move together. Judge look-alikes as a group: shuffle or drop them together.

Where look-alikes come from in real data

Two systems recording the same thing; a count and a total built from it; age and anything that rises with age. Capstone data has them too: Stage 2 is the time to look.

Why does shuffling cost more than dropping?

Shuffling prior stays (0.057, with no copy) keeps the fitted model and feeds it someone else’s count: it is misled, not just uninformed. It measures what this model relies on.

Dropping and refitting it and its copy (0.036) lets the forest lean on columns that carry part of the same signal. It measures what the column adds that nothing else can replace.

Does SHAP have the same problem?

Yes: with two look-alikes in the model, SHAP splits the credit between them. Studio question 1 tries the copy in logistic regression.

Part 2 · thin data · breaks partial dependence and ICEWere there patients here?

Run Tuesday’s partial dependence for prior stays (it stopped at 7) out to 15, and the patients run out long before the curve does.

  • Where the patients are: 96 training stays had 8 or more prior stays, 40 had 10 or more, and 8 had 15 or more.
  • In the shaded region, both curves are the model talking, not the data.

In practice: draw a curve only where there are patients (Lab 9 stops at the 99th percentile), print the counts beside it, and say so when a prediction lands where few patients were.

Why the ruler climbs and the staircase stops

Logistic regression is a ruler: every prior stay adds the same +0.27 to the log-odds, learned where the patients are, and it keeps adding past them: 58% at 10 stays, 83% at 15.

The forest is a staircase: each box needs at least 5 training patients, so the steps stop where the patients thin out, and every count beyond gets the same average, near 31%.

Didn’t Tuesday say the forest was closer?

For the 592 stays where the models disagreed, yes: 27.9% came back, against 35.8% from logistic regression and 24.4% from the forest. Studio question 2 checks both models against what happened, group by group.

Part 3 · not a cause · breaks SHAP valuesAm I describing the model, or claiming a cause?

A push says what moved the model’s score. It does not say what would change the patient.

Tuesday’s biggest disagreement, stay 18506: 12 prior stays, logistic regression 91.8%, the forest 28.5%.

Holding everything else fixed is what the model does when it moves one column, and what the hospital cannot do for a real patient. So “prior stays push the score up” is a fact about the model; “preventing earlier stays would lower this patient’s risk” is a claim nothing in the data tested.

In practice: say “raises the model’s score,” never “raises the risk.” A claim about what an action would do needs an experiment, or a causal analysis, not a SHAP value.

Two exact explanations, two models

Twelve prior stays push the forest up 13 points, and push logistic regression up +3.03 in log-odds, which alone takes Tuesday’s 10.4% typical patient to about 71%. The forest’s pushes are in chances, because it averages its trees’ chances, computed by the shap package’s TreeSHAP.

Why the forest’s typical patient is 11.6%

It is the forest’s average chance over its training patients. Logistic regression’s typical patient (Tuesday, 10.4%) is an average in log-odds turned into a chance, which comes out lower.

Before the studio · the reading · about three minutesWhich model would you hand the hospital?

The reading in three sentences: Rudin (2019)An explanation added after the fact only approximates the black box, and today showed three ways that misleads. On tables like ours, a model you can read often ties the black box: her best-known example, a few rules on age and prior offenses, predicted re-arrest about as well as COMPAS, a commercial tool used in courts. So for high-stakes decisions, when one ties, use the one you can read.
