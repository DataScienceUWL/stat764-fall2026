Part 1 · the idea, derivedFinding a better coordinate system

Suppose we have n observations, each described by p numerical features. Think of them as points in a p-dimensional feature space. The features are often correlated: colleges with high graduation rates tend to have high SAT scores.

Principal component analysisfinds a new orthonormal basis for the feature space in which the new variables are uncorrelated, ordered so that the first has the greatest variance, the second the next greatest, and so on. The new variables are the principal components.
  1. Find a better basis
  2. construct uncorrelated features
  3. order them by variance
  4. interpret the results

1 · We are looking for a new basis

Start with n observations of p standardized features, X∈ℝn×p. Each row is an observation, a vector in ℝp; each column is an original feature. We want to describe the same observations with different coordinate axes. Let the columns of Φ be an orthonormal basis of ℝp:

Φp×p=(|||φ1φ2⋯φp|||)

Each φₘ is a unit vector in ℝp, a p × 1 column, and they are at right angles to each other: ΦTΦ=I, the p × p identity.

One observation, one new coordinate. Take the three standardized college features of Part 2: SAT, graduation rate and tuition. College 52 is one row of X, a 1 × 3 row:

x52=(2.171.13−0.45)

For these colleges, the first principal component direction (found in Part 2) is the 3 × 1 column

φ1=(0.610.630.49)

College 52’s score on PC1 is the dot product:

z52,1=x52φ1=2.17(0.61)+1.13(0.63)−0.45(0.49)≈1.82

That number says where the college lies along the PC1 axis. Do the same with φ₂ for PC2, φ₃ for PC3, and for every row of X. Matrix multiplication does all of those dot products at once:

Zn×p=XΦ

Row i of Z is observation i in the new coordinates; column m of Z is the new variable PCm. The question: how should we choose Φ?

2 · We want the new features to be uncorrelated

The covariance matrix of the standardized original features is

Sp×p=1n−1XTX

and the covariance matrix of the new features is

Cov(Z)p×p=1n−1ZTZ=1n−1ΦTXTXΦ=ΦTSΦ

Uncorrelated means that covariance matrix is diagonal. So we want an orthonormal Φ with

ΦTSΦ=Λp×p

where Λ is diagonal. That is exactly what an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of S does. S is symmetric, so the spectral theorem guarantees one exists.

S is also positive semidefinite: for any vector v,

vTSv=vT(1n−1XTX)v=1n−1‖Xv‖2≥0

so its eigenvalues are real and nonnegative.

3 · The eigenvectors give us the basis

Find the eigenvectors of S,

Sφm=λma numberφm

choose them orthonormal, and arrange them so their eigenvalues decrease, λ1≥λ2≥⋯≥λp. Now put all p of these equations side by side as columns. Column m of SΦ is Sφₘ, and column m of ΦΛ is λₘφₘ, where Λ=diag(λ1,…,λp):

SΦ=ΦΛ

Multiply both sides on the right by Φᵀ. Φ is square with ΦᵀΦ = I, so Φᵀ is its inverse and ΦΦᵀ = I too:

S=ΦΛΦT

Substitute that into Cov(Z) = ΦᵀSΦ from section 2:

Cov(Z)=ΦTSΦ=ΦT(ΦΛΦT)Φ=(ΦTΦ)Λ(ΦTΦ)=IΛI=Λ

So the eigenvectors do exactly what section 2 asked for:

Cov(Z)=Λ

The columns of Z are uncorrelated, and their variances are the eigenvalues: Var(Zm)=λm. These new variables are the principal components.

4 · Why order them by variance?

Ordering by decreasing eigenvalue gives Var(Z1)≥Var(Z2)≥⋯≥Var(Zp). Then the first k components capture as much variance as any k-dimensional subspace can. That is what makes PCA useful beyond decorrelating: if the last eigenvalues are small, drop those components and lose little variance.

New variables, not new correlationsPCA does not make the original features uncorrelated; it cannot. It builds new features, each a linear combination of the original ones, whose correlations are zero. And it takes the eigenvectors of S: an arbitrary orthonormal basis would not do it.
Why the first component has the most variance (optional)

Along a unit vector w the new variable is Xw, with variance wᵀSw. Maximizing wᵀSw subject to wᵀw = 1 (a Lagrange multiplier) gives Sw = λw, and then the variance is wᵀSw = λ: the best direction is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. The second does the same at right angles to the first, and so on.

Repeated eigenvalues (optional)

If two eigenvalues are equal, any orthonormal basis of their eigenspace gives the same diagonal covariance, so the individual components are not unique. The subspace they span, and its variance, still are.

Why the eigenvalues add up to p (optional)

Write out entry (j, j) of S = ΦΛΦᵀ: row j of Φ, times Λ, times column j of Φᵀ.

Sjj=∑m=1pλmφjm2

Add these up over the p variables and swap the order of the sums. Each column φₘ is a unit vector, so its squared entries add to 1:

∑j=1pSjj=∑m=1pλm(∑j=1pφjm2)=∑m=1pλm·1=λ1+⋯+λp

On the left, each Sⱼⱼ is the variance of a standardized variable, which is 1, so the left side is p. Hence λ₁ + ⋯ + λₚ = p. Each term λₘφⱼₘ² is the squared correlation of variable j with component m: the correlation table in Part 2’s reveal “Interpreting the components”, where each row adds to 1 (a variable’s variance, shared out among the components) and each column to its eigenvalue (what a component collects from the variables).

scikit-learn stores one component per row, so Φ is pca.components_.T. Part 2 computes every one of these objects for 60 made-up colleges.

n or n − 1? (if you check the numbers)

scikit-learn mixes the two. StandardScaler divides by n (the population standard deviation), while PCA computes S, and explained_variance_, with n − 1. So after StandardScaler each column’s variance is n/(n − 1), a little above 1, and the eigenvalues add up to p · n/(n − 1): 39.01 for the 2,954 counties, not 39. The loadings and the shares of variance are the same either way. (The colleges in Part 2 are standardized with n − 1, so their eigenvalues add to exactly 3.)

The recipe in one picture (optional)
  1. Original dataX(n×p) Rows = observations, columns = variables
  2. Center, and standardize, the columnsXc Each column minus its mean, divided by its standard deviation. Part 1 writes X for this.
  3. Sample covariance matrixS=1n−1XcTXc p × p; standardized, it is the correlation matrix
  4. EigendecompositionS=ΦΛΦT Columns of Φ (eigenvectors) = loadings; Λ = diag(λ₁, …, λₚ) (eigenvalues) = the components’ variances
  5. Principal component scoresZ=XcΦ n × p: every observation in the new variables
  6. Keep the first kZk=XcΦkshare of component m=λmλ1+⋯+λp The first k columns of Φ; k = 2 gives the biplot

In practice scikit-learn gets Φ and Λ from the singular value decomposition of Xc, without forming S; the result is the same.

College 52 is well above average overall (PC1), a bargain for its academics (PC2), and its SAT runs a little ahead of its graduation rate (PC3). Keep all three columns and nothing is lost: only the description changed.

Step 5Eigenvalues: how much each new variable varies

The eigenvalues λₘ (“lambda m”), largest first, are the variances of the columns of Z (Part 1, section 3): how much the colleges vary along each component.

Each eigenvalue is a variance; standardized, they add up to 3
componentλₘ (variance)share, λₘ / 3
PC12.2173.6%

PC30.165.4%

Shares of what? Of the total variance: the variances of the standardized variables added up, 1 + 1 + 1 = 3. It is also the average squared distance of a college from the average college, in standard-deviation units. Φ only rotates the axes, so no distance changes: College 52 is 2.17² + 1.13² + 0.45² ≈ 6.2 from the average in the old coordinates and 1.81² + 1.60² + 0.59² ≈ 6.2 in the new. In the new coordinates each squared distance splits into one piece per component; averaged over the colleges, the pieces are λ₁, λ₂ and λ₃.

Three new variables, uncorrelated with each other, ordered from most to least variable.

With just two variables: the shares come from the correlation

Two standardized variables with correlation r have S = 1rr1. Its eigenvalues are 1 + r and 1 − r, with eigenvectors (1, 1)/2 and (1, −1)/2, so PC1 weighs the two equally and carries (1 + r)/2 of the variance. Your notebook’s Section 1 does this with smoking and inactivity: r = 0.79, so 90%.

The shares depend on how distance is measured

Standardizing makes a one-standard-deviation difference count the same on every variable. Change the units, or enter a look-alike variable twice, and the total and every share change with it. Studio question 2 shows how much.

Step 6Keep two columns, and look

Z₂ = XΦ₂  (the first two columns of Φ: 73.6% + 21.0% = 94.6% of the variance)

Geometrically, keeping two columns is an orthogonal projection of the 3-D cloud onto the plane spanned by PC1 and PC2. Start with the colleges and their axes, add the layers one at a time while you turn the cloud, then look straight down at the plane.

Looking straight down is the biplot. The colleges land at the rows of Z₂; the axes’ unit vectors land at the rows of Φ₂.

  • Which colleges are alike? Points close together.
  • What separates them? The arrows: SAT and graduation point almost the same way (correlation 0.83); tuition points up, along PC2.
  • How good is the picture? 94.6% of the variance lies in the plane. Tuition’s arrow has length 0.99 (its axis lies almost in the plane); SAT’s 0.74 and graduation’s 0.68 are shorter because their axes tilt out of it, along PC3.
Reading values off the biplot, and going back

A college’s score times an arrow, zᵢ · φₖ, is its value of that variable as the plane sees it. All at once: X̂ = Z₂Φ₂ᵀ = XΦ₂Φ₂ᵀ. What X̂ misses is PC3, 5.4% of the variance.

Interpreting the components (your reference for studio question 1)

To name a component: turn its loadings into correlations, name both ends, then check the name on the colleges at each end.

1 · Loadings to correlations. A loading is one entry of a unit vector, so its size depends on how many variables there are. Multiplied by λ, it becomes the correlation between that variable and the component’s scores:

corr(variablej,PCm)=φjmλm

Why the square root of λ? (optional)

Take column j of X, xⱼ (standardized: standard deviation 1), and the scores on component m, zₘ = Xφₘ (standard deviation λm). Their covariance uses Sφₘ = λₘφₘ; dividing by the two standard deviations gives the correlation:

Cov(xj,zm)=1n−1xjTXφm=(Sφm)j=λmφjmcorr(xj,zm)=λmφjm1·λm=λmφjm

The sign is arbitrary: flip a column of Φ and its two ends swap names, nothing else changes. Part 3 · the real dataThe counties: 39 measures, a few directions

One direction carries 39% of the differences between counties, and it is sickness and poverty.

The data. County Health Rankings is a yearly release from the University of Wisconsin Population Health Institute that describes the health of every US county. The 2025 file has a row for each of 3,152 counties and 90 measures. We use 39 of them, all rates, in very different units: shares (adults who smoke, 0.18 in the typical county), averages (poor physical health days a month, 4.4), dollars (median household income, $62,851), and counts per 100,000 (preventable hospital stays per 100,000 Medicare enrollees, 2,743). That is why we standardize. The numbers come from surveys, the census, Medicare records and death records, many averaged over several years. Life expectancy (75.3 years in the typical county) stays out of the PCA; Part 4 predicts it.

We use the n = 2,954 counties that have all 39 measures and life expectancy; the other 198 are Thursday’s topic. The same steps as the colleges: X is 2,954 × 39, standardized; S and Φ are 39 × 39; there are 39 eigenvalues, λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ λ₃₉.

Name: sickness and poverty. It correlates −0.80 with life expectancy, a variable the PCA never saw.

  • What it says nothing about: its correlations with % 65 and older (−0.11), housing cost burden (0.12) and air pollution (0.13) are near zero. Two counties with the same score can differ a lot on those.
  • Nearly an equal-weight average: its scores correlate 0.985 with the plain average of all 39 standardized measures, each turned to point the same way. Most measures move together, so PCA’s weights come out nearly equal.
  • Stable: across 200 bootstrap resamples of the counties, its direction barely moves (cosine with the original at least 0.999 in 95% of them). Components 4 and 5 tilt noticeably (down to about 0.93): their eigenvalues, 2.16 and 1.93, are nearly tied, so name them with care.

Components 2 and 3 are studio question 1.

Why the sign does not matter

A direction can point either way. PCA could as well have made “healthy and well off” the positive end; the loadings would all flip, and nothing else would change.

Why standardize first?

PCA looks for the largest spread in the units it is handed. Studio question 2 shows what it finds when the measures are left in their own units.

Part 4 · a useDoes compressing help a prediction?

Five components miss life expectancy by about 2.0 years, on counties from states the model never saw. Guessing the average misses by 3.6.

The model: principal components regression. Replace the 39 measures by the first k columns of Z = XΦ, the scores, and regress life expectancy on them. With k = 5:

y^=b0+b1z1+b2z2+⋯+b5z5

Fitted to all counties, b₁ = −0.72: a county one unit further along “sickness and poverty” is predicted to live 0.72 fewer years. The scores are uncorrelated, so their coefficients do not fight the way look-alike columns’ did in Meeting 20 (prior stays and its near-copy).

make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=5), LinearRegression())
  • Inside the pipeline: the scaler, Φ and the regression are learned on the training counties only. The held-out counties are scaled, projected with that Φ, and predicted.
  • Split by state: counties in one state share laws, Medicaid rules and climate, so each of the 5 folds holds out about 10 whole states, about 590 counties (the grouped split from Meeting 4, where whole patients were held out).
  • The score: RMSE, the typical miss in years on the held-out counties, averaged over the 5 folds.

Fitting PCA on every county first: a leak?

Here it moves the error by 0.004 years: PCA never sees the outcome, so there is little to leak. Keep it inside the pipeline anyway; the same habit stops the leaks that do matter (Meeting 4).
