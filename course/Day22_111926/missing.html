Before the new idea · about six minutesConnecting the dots: PCA

Try first: answer each in your head, then open it

1. Component 1 of the 39 county measures carried 39% of the variance. What was it about?

Sickness and poverty: poor or fair health, disability, physical distress, diabetes, inactivity, children in poverty. It correlated −0.80 with life expectancy.

2. Why standardize before PCA?

PCA finds the largest spread in whatever units it is handed. Left in dollars, component 1 was median household income alone: 99.6% of the variance.

3. Fitting PCA on every county before splitting: how big a leak?

Tiny, 0.004 years: PCA never sees the outcome. It goes inside the pipeline anyway, and so does today’s filling in.

Covariance PCA or correlation PCA?

Tuesday’s exit ticket asked what you would want to know about a report built on “the first principal component of 39 health measures.” Every answer asked: were the measures standardized? The right first question. Here is when the answer should be no.

PCA always finds the directions of largest spread. Standardizing first decides what “spread” means.

The two choices
the loadings are the eigenvectors ofa step of 1 in a measure means
covariance PCA: center onlyS, the covariance matrixone of its own units: a dollar, a percentage point, a day
correlation PCA: center and standardizeR=D−1SD−1, the correlation matrix (D: each measure’s standard deviation, on the diagonal)one standard deviation of that measure

The counties, unstandardized (Tuesday’s studio question 2, PCA on the raw measures): before computing, 6 of 8 of you guessed the measure with the biggest scale would take over; 2 guessed component 1 would still be about health. With income in dollars, component 1 is income alone, 99.56% of the variance. In thousands of dollars, it is preventable hospital stays alone, 99.98%. The counties did not change; the units chose the answer. Standardized, component 1 is the same sickness-and-poverty direction, 38.8%, in either unit.

When to choose which
your measureschoosebecause
different units: dollars, percents, dayscorrelationotherwise the units pick component 1, as above
one unit, and the size of each spread means something: sensors measuring one quantity, pixel brightness, subscores on one scalecovariancestandardizing throws away which measures really vary more
one unit, but some measures barely move and are mostly noisecovariancestandardizing blows that noise up to variance 1
  • Equal spread in is not equal weight out: after standardizing, the correlations still set the loadings.
  • In scikit-learn: PCA() alone is covariance PCA (it centers, never scales); make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA()) is correlation PCA, the one we used Tuesday.
Why: standardizing changes how distance is measured

Covariance PCA measures the distance between counties i and k in the original units; correlation PCA divides each measure’s difference by its standard deviation sj:

d2(xi,xk)=∑j=1p(xij−xkj)2
d2(xi,xk)=∑j=1p(xij−xkj)2sj2

So after standardizing, a one-standard-deviation difference in income counts the same as one in any other measure. Two uncorrelated measures with variances 16 and 1 show the difference: covariance PCA’s component 1 is the first measure, with 16/17 ≈ 94% of the variance. Correlation PCA sees R=I: every direction carries 50%, and there is no single component 1.

The totals differ too. The eigenvalues of R add up to p (every diagonal entry is 1), so a component’s share is λm/p. Those of S add up to the sum of the measures’ variances. And the two sets of loadings are not rescaled copies of each other: they are eigenvectors of two different matrices.

Part 1 · the readmission dataThe A1C test nobody ordered

Stays with no A1C test came back more often than stays with any result, normal or high.

The A1C is a blood test of average blood sugar over three months. It is blank for 83% of the 99,343 stays, because nobody ordered it.

What the test found barely matters; whether anyone ordered it does. So the blank is information.

Drop the untested stays and 16.9% are left: younger, diabetes-first patients who came back 9.9% of the time, not the hospital’s 11.4%. A model fitted on them is about a different group.

How did our models handle this blank since Meeting 9?

As its own category: the encoder turned the blank into nan, a column that is 1 when no test was done. That kept the information. Filling in a typical result would have erased it.

Before filling any blank, ask
  1. Why is it blank: “none”, not yet known at prediction time, or truly missing?
  2. Does the blank say something about the outcome?
  3. If you drop rows, who are they?

Part 2 · reading: blanks we make ourselvesWhy is it blank? Three reasons

For an average, dropping blanks is safe only when they are random. Filling from other columns recovers only what those columns know.

MCAR · missing completely at random
The blank has nothing to do with anything. A lab machine was down that week.
MAR · missing at random
Whether it is blank depends on other columns you have, not on the hidden value. Diabetes patients get the A1C test twice as often, so the blanks are not random overall. But if, among patients with the same diagnosis and age, who got tested has nothing to do with their actual blood sugar, the blanks are MAR. (The name misleads: random only within groups you can see.)
MNAR · missing not at random
The blank depends on the missing value itself, or on something the data do not record. A county does not report a rate when the rate is bad.

The test: take the unemployment rate of the 2,954 counties from Meeting 21, hide 30% of the values in each of the three ways, then try both fixes. We know the truth, so we can check.

How the blanks were made: each county gets a chance of being hidden, then a weighted coin flip
each county’s chance of being hiddenwhat decides it
MCAR30% for every countynothing
MARabout 3% in the least-poor tenth of counties, rising to about 81% in the poorest tenth; 30% on averagethe county’s child poverty rate, which stays visible
MNARabout 4% in the tenth with the lowest unemployment, rising to about 77% in the highest; 30% on averagethe county’s own unemployment rate: the value being hidden
The formula behind the chances

Each county’s chance is 1 / (1 + e−(2z + c)), where z is the county’s standardized child poverty (MAR) or standardized unemployment (MNAR), and c is set so that the chances average 30%. The flips hid 834, 881 and 910 counties.

For a model, dropping does something else too: it changes who the model is about, as the untested stays in Part 1 showed.

Can the data tell you which reason you have?

Only partly. Under MAR and under MNAR the blanked counties are poorer (26% and 23% of children in poverty, against 16% and 17%). So the data can show the blanks are not random, but not which of the two they are. That takes knowing how the data were collected: ask whoever made it.

What does “fill from the other 38” do?

IterativeImputer predicts the blank column from the other columns with a regression, fills the blanks, and repeats until the fills settle. It recovers what the other columns know about the missing value.

One more kind: censored values

Sometimes a value is not missing but cut off: you know it lies beyond a limit, not where. A lab result below the test’s detection limit is reported as “< 0.5”. A patient still out of the hospital when the data end has “no readmission yet”, which only means “not within the days we watched”. Censoring is a cousin of MNAR: whether a value is cut off depends on the value itself, but you also know which side of the limit it is on.

  • Don’t fill it with a typical value: you already know it is beyond the limit.
  • Don’t drop it: you would drop exactly the extreme cases.
  • Keep what you know: the limit plus a 0/1 “cut off” column, or a method built for it (survival analysis for times, Tobit regression for capped values).

In your capstone: a training row whose outcome had not happened yet by your cutoff date is censored. That is why every training row’s outcome must be known before the test period starts.

When something else stops the event: competing risks

A patient in a cancer study who dies in an accident can never die of the cancer: that is a competing risk, not a blank. Treating such deaths as censored is fine for the rate of the event among those still alive; for “what fraction will have it by time t” use competing-risks methods (cumulative incidence, Fine–Gray). In a fixed-window prediction, change the outcome instead: “readmitted or died within 30 days”, or three outcomes. Never count the deaths as successes, and always report them beside the event.

It is in our readmission data: we dropped stays that ended in death, but a patient who dies at home within 30 days is recorded as “not readmitted”. Deaths after discharge are not recorded, so all we can do is say so.

For prediction, one default covers the first three: fill inside the pipeline and add the “was blank” column (Part 3 shows both). That matters because the data cannot tell MAR from MNAR. The real risk is the blanks’ cause changing after training, say a clinic that starts testing everyone.

Part 3 · reading: in practiceFilling in honestly

A fill is learned, so it goes inside the pipeline. And a fill is a guess, not a measurement.

1. The fill goes inside the pipeline

  • Inside the pipeline: the medians, or the imputer’s regressions, come from the training rows only, like the scaler and PCA.
  • Keep the blank as information: add_indicator=True adds a 0/1 “was blank” column: the number-column version of the A1C’s “not measured”.
make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True),
              StandardScaler(), LinearRegression())

Part 2’s better fill works the same way. IterativeImputer predicts each blank from the other 38 measures; inside a pipeline, on Part 2’s MAR blanks, it lets the model predict life expectancy for all 2,954 counties, the 881 with unemployment hidden included: RMSE 1.73 years, about what Meeting 21’s regression on all 39 measures got with nothing hidden.

2. A fill is a guess: how sure is it?

Every fill so far puts one number in each blank: the median, or the imputer’s best guess. From then on the model treats that number exactly like a measured one. But a guess from the other measures can be off, and a single number cannot show by how much.

To see it, ask the imputer for plausible values instead of its best guess. sample_posterior=True adds a random amount of its own uncertainty to each prediction. Run it five times with different seeds and each blank gets five plausible fills: where they agree, the guess is solid; where they scatter, it is not.

draws = [IterativeImputer(sample_posterior=True, random_state=s)
             .fit_transform(X_mar) for s in range(5)]

Part 2’s MAR test is the place to look, because there we know the hidden values. Three of the 881 counties:

Read it: Autauga’s single fill says 3.60; the truth is 2.19, and the draws run from 1.65 to 5.05. The draws admit the imputer does not know; the single fill looks as sure as a measurement. (Barbour’s single fill happens to land on the truth: 4.40 against 4.43.) Across all 881 counties, one county’s draws typically spread ±0.64 points, about half of how much unemployment varies between counties (±1.18).

Yet averages barely move: the average over all counties is 3.58 to 3.61 in every draw. So a single fill is fine for an average, or for a model fitted on many rows; a statement about one row, or a margin of error, has to carry the uncertainty forward.

What is multiple imputation?

Fill the blanks several times with different draws, fit the model on each filled copy, and report how much the answer moves. That spread is the uncertainty a single fill hides.
