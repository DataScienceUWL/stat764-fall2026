Growing a forest

On Tuesday a single tree was easy to read and hard to trust: change the sample a little and its questions change. Today’s fix sounds too simple to work. Grow many trees, each on a slightly different sample of the same data, and average what they say.

ResampleDraw a new sample of the training cases, the same size, with replacement.
GrowGrow a full tree on that sample. A random forest also limits each split to a few random columns.
AverageRepeat a few hundred times, and predict with the average of all the trees.

Part 1The same twenty patients

These are Tuesday’s 20 made-up patients: prior hospital stays, days in the hospital, and whether they came back within 30 days. Seven of the 20 did. On Tuesday one tree, grown all the way, put every one of them on the right side by fencing off single patients. That tree had memorized them. Here we grow hundreds of trees instead.

Step 1A bootstrap sample

A bootstrap sample draws 20 patients from the 20, with replacement: after each draw the patient goes back in, so some are drawn twice or three times and some not at all. It is a new data set of the same size that the original could plausibly have been. Grow a tree on it, then draw again.

Step 2Average the trees: bagging

Each tree’s mistakes are partly the luck of its sample. Predicting with the average of many trees cancels much of that luck and keeps what the trees agree on. Growing trees on bootstrap samples and averaging them is called bagging, short for bootstrap aggregating. Move the slider and watch the map settle.

One tree gives each patient either 0% or 100%: its leaves are pure. The average of many gives shades in between, and the map stops jumping once there are a few dozen trees. Nothing about any single tree improved. The average did.

Step 3Make the trees disagree: a random forest

Averaging cancels mistakes best when the trees make different mistakes. Bagged trees all see every column, so they tend to ask the same strong first question and make similar mistakes. A random forest changes one thing: at every split, a tree may only choose among a random few of the columns. With our two columns, that means one column picked at random at each split. Each tree gets a little worse, and the trees become much less alike.

How a forest picks its columns

  • A fresh draw at every split. Each time a tree is about to split a group, it draws a random handful of the columns, runs Tuesday’s search (every cut, weighted Gini) on those columns only, and makes the best split it finds. The next split, even in the same tree, draws again, so one tree mixes many different draws.
  • How many: max_features. scikit-learn’s default for classification is the square root of the number of columns, rounded down. A third of the columns is a common choice for regression. All of the columns (max_features=None or 1.0) is bagging.
  • If no drawn column can split the group, the tree keeps drawing until one can.

With our two columns, √2 rounds down to 1: one random column at each split, which is what the right-hand map used. Part 2 shows what the same rule does with 105 columns.

Step 4Out-of-bag: a test set that comes free

A bootstrap sample of 20 leaves out about a third of the patients: on average (1 − 1/20)20 ≈ 36%, and (1 − 1/n)n ≈ 1/e ≈ 37% for any large n. So every patient was not seen by about a third of the trees. Predict each patient with only those trees, and you have an honest prediction for every training patient without setting any data aside: the out-of-bag prediction.

Step 5Which columns does a forest use?

A forest of 200 trees cannot be read like one flowchart. Two numbers summarize which columns it leans on:

  • Impurity importance (feature_importances_): how much each column’s splits lowered the Gini impurity, added up over every split of every tree, on the training data. It is free, and biased: a count with hundreds of different values offers far more places to split than a yes/no column, so it wins splits by luck as well as by signal.
  • Permutation importance: shuffle one column among held-out patients, which breaks its link to the outcome, and measure how much the AUC drops. Slower, and measured where it counts.

To see the difference, give every patient one more column of pure noise: the numbers 1 to 20 in random order, like a record number, which says nothing about coming back. Grow a random forest of 200 trees on the three columns, and compute both kinds of importance.

What this shows. Impurity importance hands the noise column about as much credit as days in the hospital. With 20 different values it offers 19 places to cut, and fully grown trees use them to finish purifying their small leaves. Permutation importance, measured on patients each tree never saw, gives the noise column less than nothing and shows prior stays doing nearly all the work.

Neither says what would happen to a patient if you changed that column. Importance describes what the model uses to predict, not what causes a readmission. Studio question 3 puts both kinds to the test.

Step 3 With 200 trees each: bagging reaches a test AUC of 0.629, the random forest 0.651. The first questions show why.

Why only 24? After one-hot encoding, the training sample has 105 columns: the 8 counts and 97 category columns. The forest’s default lets each split consider √105, rounded down: 10 of them. Prior stays is one column of 105, so it is in a given split’s draw only 10/105 = 9.5% of the time. At the root of 200 trees, about 19 would even have it to choose. When it is drawn it usually wins; when it is not, the tree opens with the best of what it drew. Twenty-four is within ordinary luck of 19. Draw a few splits’ columns yourself:

The one-hot wrinkle. The forest draws encoded columns, not variables. Medical specialty’s 30 columns put at least one of them in about 97% of draws, but each asks a narrow question (“cardiology or not?”) and rarely wins a split. How often a variable is considered depends on how many categories it has, not on how useful it is.

Step 4 One bootstrap sample of the training stays leaves out 36.6% of them. Predicting each training stay with only the trees that never saw it gives an out-of-bag AUC of 0.650, against 0.651 on the held-out test patients. The free estimate and the held-out one agree.

One caution the notebook raises: a bootstrap sample draws stays, not patients, so a patient with three stays can be in a tree’s sample for one stay and out of it for another. Studio question 2 checks whether that matters here.

See it in Scikit-Learn

Thursday’s forest, written out with every setting this page has been about. make_pipe is the notebook’s preprocessing (fill and scale the counts, one-hot the categories) with the model at the end.

from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

forest = make_pipe(RandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=200,       # Step 2: how many trees to average
    bootstrap=True,         # Step 1: each tree gets its own bootstrap sample of rows (the default)
    max_features="sqrt",    # Step 3: each split may use a random sqrt(p) of the columns: 10 of 105 here
                            #   max_features=None lets every split use every column: that is bagging
    min_samples_leaf=5,     # how far each tree grows: no leaf smaller than 5 (Part 1 grows all the way: 1)
    oob_score=True,         # Step 4: keep each row's out-of-bag prediction
    n_jobs=-1,              # the trees are independent, so they grow in parallel on every core
    random_state=0,         # the same samples and draws, so the same forest, every run
)).fit(X_fit, y_fit)

model = forest["model"]                                # the fitted forest inside the pipeline
p_test = forest.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]            # each test patient: the average of the 200 trees' leaf shares
oob = model.oob_decision_function_[:, 1]               # each training stay, from only the trees that never saw it
print(roc_auc_score(y_fit, oob))                       # out-of-bag AUC: 0.650 (test AUC: 0.651)
print(model.oob_score_)                                # 0.8845: accuracy at a 50% cutoff, not AUC
print(model.estimators_[0].max_features_)              # 10 columns considered at every split
tree_17 = model.estimators_[17]                        # any one of the 200, a DecisionTreeClassifier
importance = model.feature_importances_                # Step 5: impurity importance, one per encoded column

The tools the studio uses

  • scores(probs) takes a model’s predicted chances for the test patients and returns the AUC, the Brier score, the average predicted chance, and the calibration slope (1 means the spread of the probabilities is right; below 1, too extreme; above 1, too timid).
  • Cross-validation grouped by patient: cv=GroupKFold(5) with groups=groups_fit, so all of a patient’s stays fall in the same fold.
  • Out-of-bag predictions belong to the training stays, so score them against y_fit. The forest’s oob_score_ is accuracy, not AUC.
  • Permutation importance on the whole pipeline gives one number per original column, in the order NUM + CAT.
