Before the new idea · about eight minutesPicking a model when the scores tie

Five steps, in this order. Each one would have caught something in Thursday’s recommendations.

Picking among models that score about the same
stepaskon Thursday: the slip, and what to write instead
1 · Start from the decisionDoes the decision use the chances themselves, or only the order? The $150 visit goes to every stay with a chance of at least 0.169, so the chances must be right (the next section shows how to check). A nurse who reviews the top 5% uses only the order.

Slipped: most scorecards wrote down a calibration slope, but few said what it meant for the visits.

Instead: “TabICL’s chances run low, 9.8% said against 11.1% who came back, so at 0.169 it sends too few visits.”

2 · Is a gap real?Redraw the test patients many times, score both models on each redraw, and read the middle 95% of the gaps (the paired bootstrap). If that range includes 0, it is a tie. Do it in dollars, the decision’s own unit.

Slipped: some picks rested on a dollar lead that their own gap table called noise.

Instead: “TabICL leads by $591 per 1,000 stays, but the middle 95% runs from −$260 to +$1,519. On money, it is a tie.”

3 · Fix the chances, then compare againChances that run high, low or too extreme can be fixed after fitting (Meeting 12’s calibration fix). The order of the patients cannot. So a flaw you can fix should not decide on its own.

Slipped: a calibration slope below 1 was read as “chances too high.”

Instead: “TabICL’s slope of 0.85 means its chances are too extreme; its average, 9.8% against 11.1%, means they run low. Both can be fixed.”

4 · Stability, explanation, costWhat comes with the model and no fix changes: does the list of patients to visit stay the same when you refit? Can you explain one score to a nurse? What does it cost to run?

Slipped: one scorecard put the net dollars under “cost,” which means running and keeping the model.

Instead: “Logistic regression fits in under a second on a laptop. TabICL needs a GPU: about 4 seconds there, about 5 minutes on 24 CPU cores.”

5 · Still tied?Take the simplest model that does the job, and name a result that would change your mind.

Slipped: most picks named no such result, or only a general worry.

Instead: “I would switch to the forest if a refit kept as much of its top-10% list as logistic regression does: 83%, against about two-thirds.”

In practice

  1. Write the decision in one sentence before you look at any scores: who acts, on which patients, at what cost.
  2. Compare every model with the simplest one, on the same test patients, and write every gap with its middle 95%.
  3. Learn any fix on patients the model was not fitted on and judge it on others; then redo step 2. A fix you keep is part of the model from then on.
  4. Refit on a few training samples and count how much of the list to visit stays the same.
  5. Write the recommendation in the order of the steps, ending with the result that would change it.
Real is not the same as big

TabICL’s AUC lead over logistic regression was real: +0.007, with the middle 95% from +0.003 to +0.012. It was too small to show up in dollars. A gap can pass step 2 in AUC and fail it in the decision’s unit.

So which model should we have picked?

Any of the four, if the five steps get you there and you name the result that would change your mind. There is no hidden right answer. Whether fixing TabICL’s chances pays is Lab 8, due tonight; we take it up Thursday.

Before the new idea · about five minutesReading calibration: one average and one slope are not enough

Calibration asks: when the model says 20%, do about 20% come back?

Reliability table
Sort the stays by the model’s chance and cut them into groups, such as tenths. For each group, put the average chance beside the share who actually came back. A calibrated model’s two columns match in every group.
Level
Do the chances run high or low overall? Check: the average chance against the share who came back. Logistic regression: 11.5% against 11.1%.
Spread
Are the chances too extreme or too timid? Check: the calibration slope. 1 is right. Below 1 is too extreme: the high chances too high and the low ones too low. Above 1 is too timid. Logistic regression: 0.88, slightly too extreme.

Neither summary looks where the decision is made. A visit pays when more than 16.9% of patients like this one come back, so the $150 visit needs only the reliability table’s rows near 0.169. Logistic regression’s:

Each summary, fooled

Two altered versions of logistic regression’s chances, made by shifting or stretching every patient’s log-odds as Meeting 11 did. The order of the patients never changes, so all three have AUC 0.649. 11.1% came back.

  • “The average matches, so the chances are right.” The third version matches to the decimal, yet its top tenth says 38.1% when 24.4% came back.
  • “A slope of 1 means calibrated.” The second version’s slope is exactly 1.00 and every chance is too low. It skips stays where 19.1% came back and earns $1,619 less per 1,000.
  • “A slope below 1 means the chances are too high.” It means too extreme: too high at the top and too low at the bottom. High or low overall is the level.

In practice: report the average and the slope, then read the reliability table near the decision’s line, always on patients the model was not fitted on.

Why the third version barely loses money

Its chances are wrong at the ends but close near 0.169 (just above the line it says 20.3% and 17.3% came back), and the visit only looks at the line. A use that reads the top chances, like telling a patient “38%,” would be badly misled.

Is logistic regression’s slope of 0.88 a problem?

It is slightly too extreme: its top tenth says 27.8% when 24.4% came back. Near 0.169 it is close (the two rows above), so the visit barely feels it.

Where TabICL fits

TabICL has both flaws: a slope of 0.85 (too extreme) and an average of 9.8% against 11.1% (too low). Whether fixing it pays is Lab 8, due tonight.

Part 1 · the questionWhich columns does the model use?

The standard answer is permutation importance: shuffle one column among the test stays, and if the AUC falls, the model relied on it.

What this does not tell you

How the prediction moves with the column (Part 2), why one patient got their score (Part 3), and whether the column causes readmission: it describes this model, not the hospital. Thursday shows how two look-alike columns can make both seem unimportant.

Why are the importance numbers so small?

They are falls in AUC. The whole model is only 0.15 above a coin flip (0.649 against 0.5), and each of the top two columns accounts for about a third of that.

Part 2 · the questionHow does the chance move as prior stays change?

The standard answer is partial dependence: set everyone’s prior stays to 0, then 1, then 2, and predict each time.

Partial dependence
The model’s average predicted chance when every patient’s value of one column is set to the same number.
ICE: individual conditional expectation
One patient’s predicted chance as one column changes, with everything else about the patient held fixed. Partial dependence is the average of the ICE curves.

On average the chance climbs from 9.5% at no prior stays to 38.9% at seven. But the patients spread out: at seven, the middle 90% run from 24% to 56%, because their other columns differ.

How one patient’s line is made

Take stay 710 from Part 3, who had 2 prior stays. Keep everything else about them, change only prior stays, and ask the model each time:

predicted chance20.1%24.7%29.9%35.8%42.1%48.7%55.4%61.8%

At 2, their real value, the line passes through their actual prediction, 29.9%. Do this for 200 patients and you get the thin lines; average them and you get the thick one.

How to read the plot

  • The thick line’s slope is how much the model’s chance moves with the column, on average. Steep: the model leans on it. Flat: it does not.
  • The thin lines are single patients. Parallel lines mean the column moves everyone the same way. Lines that fan out mean the effect differs from patient to patient.
  • A line’s height comes from the patient’s other columns: a line that starts high belongs to a patient who is high-risk for other reasons.
  • It is a what-if about the model: what would this model predict if this patient had had five prior stays? Not what would have happened to the patient.
Why logistic regression’s lines fan out

Logistic regression gives every patient the same push per prior stay in log-odds, so in log-odds its lines are parallel. Converted to chances, the same push moves a high-risk patient further than a low-risk one, so the lines fan out. A forest can fan out for a second reason: it can learn that prior stays matter more for some patients than for others.

Where partial dependence can mislead

Setting everyone to seven prior stays includes patients for whom that combination is rare or never happens. The model answers anyway, and the average includes those answers. Thursday is about what happens where few real patients look like that.

Part 3 · the questionWhy did this patient get 29.9%?

The standard answer is SHAP values: start from a typical patient and add each column’s push.

SHAP: SHapley Additive exPlanations
Each column’s push on one patient’s prediction, measured from a typical patient. The pushes add up exactly to that patient’s score.

    Test stay 710: 2 prior stays, 10 days in the hospital, aged 80 to 90, and discharged to another inpatient care facility instead of home (discharge code 5). That discharge adds the most.

    How SHAP works

    1. Start from a typical patient: every column at its training average. Counts take their average (0.63 prior stays), categories their shares (60% discharged home). Nobody actually looks like this. The model gives this patient −2.16 in log-odds, which is 10.4%.
    2. Each column’s push is its coefficient × (this patient’s value − the average value), in log-odds. A category column is several 0/1 columns inside the model, one per category, and its push is the sum of theirs. Plus raises the chance, minus lowers it.
    3. The pushes add up exactly to this patient’s score: −2.16 + 1.31 = −0.85 in log-odds, which is 29.9%.

    For a forest or a boosted model a column’s push depends on the other columns, so there is no single formula. SHAP starts from the model’s average score over real patients, switches the columns to this patient’s values one at a time, and averages each column’s change over every order of switching. The pushes still add up exactly. The shap package’s TreeSHAP does this quickly for trees, and it is in our environment for Thursday.

    How to read it

    • Most columns barely matter for this patient: 9 of the 20 push by less than 0.03.
    • It explains this one prediction, and it describes the model: say “being discharged to another facility raises this patient’s score,” not “it raises this patient’s risk.” Patients sent to another facility tend to be sicker; the model learned the link, not a cause.
    Where the name comes from

    Lloyd Shapley’s game theory has a fair way to split a team’s payout among its players: add the players in every possible order, record what each one adds when it joins, and average. Here the players are the columns and the payout is this patient’s score minus the typical patient’s. For logistic regression every order gives a column the same push, which is why step 2 is a simple formula.

    Why the pushes are in log-odds

    The model adds things up in log-odds. Converted to chances, a push depends on the order you add it in: the discharge to another facility adds 8.8 points if it comes first, and 11.2 if it comes after the prior stays. In log-odds it is +0.72 either way.

    SHAP or permutation importance?

    Permutation importance gives one number per column for the whole model: how much AUC is lost without it. SHAP gives one number per column for each patient. Average the size of the SHAP values over many patients and you get another overall ranking, usually similar but not identical.

    Why is the typical patient 10.4% when the average chance is 11.5%?

    They are two different averages. The typical patient’s score is the average log-odds, turned into a chance; the 11.5% averages the chances themselves. The S-curve bends, so the first comes out lower. SHAP adds in log-odds, so it starts from the log-odds one.
